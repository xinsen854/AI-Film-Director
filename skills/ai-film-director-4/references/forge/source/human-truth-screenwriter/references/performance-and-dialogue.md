# Performance and dialogue

How interior life becomes visible behavior, and how dialogue sounds like people instead of like writing. Read before drafting or rewriting scenes.

## The principle

An actor cannot play a feeling. They can play an intention against an obstacle, and a body doing something while wanting something else. Emotion becomes visible when the body's management of it fails slightly — the held face that cracks for a beat, the steady hand that puts the cup down too carefully.

Write the behavior that leaks, not the emotion that leaks through it. "Grief" is unplayable. A man who keeps refolding the same shirt is grief.

Restraint governs everything below. These are a vocabulary for choosing the minimum sufficient playable behavior, not a checklist or target count. A page thick with prescribed blinks is worse than a page with none — it insults the actor and buries the scene.

## Face

The face is where suppression is visible. Write it when the character is failing to control it, or controlling it so hard the control itself shows.

Useful and playable: where the eyes go and when they leave; the delay before a reaction; a smile that arrives late or stays past its moment; the swallow before speaking; a jaw that sets; the blink that resets a face into composure; looking at someone's mouth instead of their eyes; the moment of realizing one is being watched.

What is happening beneath, which you design but do not write: the character deciding whether to be seen feeling this.

Do not write emotion adjectives on faces — "she looks devastated," "his face fills with rage." That is the actor's job and you have taken it. Write what the camera would see: she doesn't look up from the sink.

Never itemize a face. One selected facial beat in a scene lands; four make a diagram.

## Body

Behavior under pressure, roughly in order of usefulness:

- **What the hands do** while the mouth does something else. Hands are the most reliable channel — they tidy, grip, hide, fidget, still themselves deliberately.
- **Distance and orientation.** Who moves closer, who angles away, who stays in the doorway rather than entering, who sits when standing would be a challenge.
- **Doing a task through the scene.** Cooking, packing, work. The task absorbs what cannot be said and gives the actor something to fail at.
- **Interruptions to the body's routine** — a sentence abandoned mid-way, a hand stopped halfway to a phone, eating that stops.
- **Physical competence as defense.** Becoming busy and capable so as not to feel. Especially useful for the character who cannot be seen to break.
- **Breath.** The held breath, the exhale that gives away a decision.
- **Touch, and its refusal.** What kind, how long, who withdraws first.

Status lives in the body: who waits, who is permitted to sit, who touches whose things, who apologizes with posture.

## Voice as physical event

Voice is performance, not just words. Volume dropping when the stakes rise. Speaking faster to prevent a reply. The flat register of someone reciting something rehearsed. A laugh in the wrong place. Talking to a third person rather than the one who matters. Going silent mid-argument — the strongest available move and easy to overuse.

Silence is a written choice. Mark the beat where someone does not answer, and make clear it is refusal rather than absence.

## Objects, space, and business

Give the scene something physical to happen through. A recurring object accumulates meaning from events and should not be explained. A space changes character as events change what happens in it.

Business — the small practical activity of a scene — is what keeps dialogue from becoming two heads exchanging positions. It also provides legitimate interruption, so characters can avoid a subject without announcing avoidance.

## Dialogue that sounds human

**Use dual-purpose dialogue when pressure creates subtext.** Many lines benefit from a practical surface purpose the character would admit to and a private purpose they would not, but not every line needs a hidden agenda. Directness can itself be a strategy, especially when a character is tired, cornered, intimate, procedural, or finally choosing clarity.

**People do not say what they mean.** They approach it, test the ground, and retreat. They ask about something adjacent. They answer a different question than the one asked. They make a joke to check whether the subject is survivable.

**Do not default to diagnostic self-explanation.** Characters are often partial, defensive or strategically inaccurate about themselves, but earned self-recognition is allowed when the story has prepared it and the act of saying it changes the situation. Prefer lived language over therapeutic summary; direct articulation should be specific to this person, moment and relationship rather than a generic explanation of motive.

**Concrete beats abstract.** People argue about the dishes, the money, the time of the flight. The real subject stays under it. Let the small thing carry the large one and do not have anyone point out that it is doing so.

**Voice is strategy, not vocabulary.** Distinguish characters by how they handle pressure — one deflects with humor, one goes procedural and precise, one asks questions, one over-explains, one gets quieter. Two characters with different word choices but the same defensive strategy are the same character. Test: cover the cues and read the lines. If you cannot tell who is speaking, the problem is strategy, not diction.

**Real speech is untidy.** Interruption, overlap, unfinished sentences, repetition under stress, filler that reveals hesitation. Use it selectively; transcribed speech is unreadable, but perfectly formed sentences are unplayable.

**Let people be funny, petty, hungry, and bored** inside serious scenes. Grief includes irritation about parking.

**Test the last line.** Many exchanges run one line past their dramatic endpoint. Cut a final line when it merely summarizes meaning already carried by action or consequence; keep it when the line itself is a decision, rupture, lie, promise, practical instruction or other event that changes what follows.

For Chinese-language dialogue, all of the above holds, and register does extra work: forms of address, whether someone uses a name at all, the shift between dialect and standard speech, honorifics dropped or restored, the difference between what is said to family and what is said in front of outsiders. Do not translate English rhythms; write speech as it is actually spoken in the setting.

## 中文短剧的额外检查

短剧中的一句台词可以同时完成任务、关系测试和信息更新，但不要把每句都写成金句。检查：

- 台词的表层任务是什么（收钱、问路、递物、安排工作、确认消息）；
- 隐含目的是什么（保面子、试探、推责、求助、拒绝欠情）；
- 关键信息是否由人物语域承载：称谓、礼貌层级、古今用语、行业词和省略方式是否符合身份与关系；
- 删除解释性半句后，动作和后果是否仍可理解；
- 同一概念再次出现时，是否因当前关系、知识差或代价变化而改变含义。

“网感”、爆点、金句和每分钟密度只有在用户明确指定平台或传播目标时才作为条件式适配项。即使是竖屏短剧，也不要自动使用夸张表演、连续反转或现代网络梗。优先保留人物的防御策略、具体任务和下一集状态。

## Writing it on the page

Behavior goes in the action lines, in present tense, observable, concise. Use the minimum sufficient physical beats at the moments that carry the turn; a scene may need none, one, or several depending on what the camera must actually witness.

Parentheticals only when delivery would otherwise be materially ambiguous. Not for emotion, not for emphasis, not for what the line already says.

Do not write camera direction, shot size, or lens data unless a shot is itself the story event. "She doesn't look up" is direction enough.

Do not annotate your own choices. The page shows behavior; the reasoning stays in the ledger.

## Failure checks

Before delivering scenes, check:

- Any face given an emotion adjective instead of an action.
- Any character who states their own motive or the theme.
- Any exchange where every line's surface purpose equals its private purpose.
- Any scene where nobody's hands are doing anything and nothing is being done.
- Any two characters whose lines are interchangeable once the cues are hidden.
- Any parenthetical that repeats the line's evident meaning.
- Any scene whose final line explains the scene.
- Any run of pages where physical behavior is described for the focal character only, while everyone else in the room has no body.
