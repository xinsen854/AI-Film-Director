# Upgrade pipeline

The default path when a user supplies an outline, treatment, or draft script and wants better material back. Material in, pages out.

## Contents

1. Intake
2. Diagnosis before drafting
3. The upgrade decision
4. Drafting
5. Delivery and continuation
6. What not to do

## Intake

Read the supplied material completely before judging it. Establish, from the material itself rather than by asking:

- **Format and scale:** feature, series episode, short, animation, branded piece. Page or scene count present versus implied.
- **Stage:** logline, outline, beat sheet, treatment, partial draft, complete draft. This decides whether you are building structure or repairing pages.
- **What is locked:** names, ending, canon, required lines, any element the user described as fixed. Absent a statement, treat supplied proper nouns, the ending, and the premise as SUPPORTED-and-preferably-kept, and everything else as open.
- **Language:** deliver in the user's language, including the pages.
- **Ambition mismatch:** what the material appears to want to be versus what it currently is. The gap is the work.

Ask a question only when the answer changes what you build: runtime when it cannot be inferred, whether the ending may change when the ending is the problem, or whether a series episode has neighbors you have not been shown. One question, not a form.

State bounded assumptions and proceed. "Reading this as a ~45-minute streaming episode, first of a season" beats an interrogation.

## Diagnosis before drafting

Run the internal analysis before writing a line. This is the step that makes the upgrade something other than a prose polish. Do not deliver this as the answer; it is apparatus.

1. **Build the human engine** for each principal from `SKILL.md` — dilemma, want, need, defense, self-story, relationship debt, relational tension. Where the material is silent, invent and mark it PROPOSED.
2. **Read the emotional surface.** Take the two or three heaviest events. For each: who is pressed first, who is displaced along which relationship line, how their responses differ in kind, who is close enough to move and does not. This is where most supplied material is thinnest — outlines almost always press one character and leave the rest as furniture.
3. **Locate the earliest responsible layer.** Weak pages usually fail upstream of themselves. A flat climax is often a premise that never gave the opposing force a case. Repair the cause, not the symptom.
4. **Name identification anchors** and check whether the material's reveals land differently across them, or produce one uniform reaction.
5. **Run `failure-patterns.md`** against the material. Declarative emotion, instant reconciliation, interchangeable voice, invisible action, consequence-free harm.
6. **Mark VERIFY items** — procedure, medicine, law, history, living culture.

## The upgrade decision

Decide, and be able to state in one line, what kind of upgrade this is:

- **Structural:** the causal chain is broken or repetitive. Scenes must be merged, resequenced, or built. Highest-leverage and most invasive.
- **Surface:** the structure holds but events press only one person. Add transmission, differential, and slack — usually without adding plot.
- **Performance:** structure and surface hold but the pages are unplayable — emotion is stated, bodies are absent, dialogue is interchangeable. Read `performance-and-dialogue.md`.
- **Compression:** everything works but too slowly, or the same dramatic function repeats.

Most real material needs two of these. Do all of them silently; name the primary one in the delivery note so the user knows what changed and why.

Prefer deepening what is there over adding events. Do not reach for death, illness, betrayal, or childhood trauma to fix weak emotion — test relationship history, withheld information, timing, and choice first.

## Drafting

Write complete scenes in professional screenplay form, in the user's language, per `delivery-contracts.md`. Every scene carries the causal chain and shows physical behavior rather than emotional labels.

Where the supplied material had a scene that works, keep its function and voice; do not rewrite for the sake of visible difference. Where it had a scene that repeats another, merge them and say so.

Run the ten passes from `SKILL.md` before delivering. The transmission pass and the restraint pass catch the most.

## Delivery and continuation

Deliver in this order:

1. **The pages.** Complete scenes, correct format, user's language.
2. **A short change note.** Primary upgrade type, the two or three structural decisions that matter, anything LOCKED you had to work around, unresolved VERIFY items. Keep it to a paragraph or a short list — the pages are the answer.
3. **Optional analysis only if asked.**

For material too long to deliver at once, deliver a labeled scene range and maintain the scene ledger from `workflows.md`. Never restart at scene 1. For a series episode, `series-continuity.md` governs what must survive into the next episode.

Offer the next unit explicitly at the end — the next scene range, the next episode, or the next act — so continuation is one word for the user rather than a re-brief.

## What not to do

- Do not return analysis when the user wanted pages.
- Do not return an outline when the user supplied an outline. Upgrading an outline means writing the scenes, unless the user asked for a better outline.
- Do not silently discard supplied characters, names, or the ending. If the ending is the problem, propose the change and say why; do not swap it out quietly.
- Do not put the emotional surface on the page. Pressure points, transmission lines, anchors, and tension states are design apparatus. On the page they appear only as behavior.
- Do not pad to a page count. Deliver fewer, fuller scenes and continue.
