# Reaction Policy

Reaction is dramatic information, not decoration.

## Useful patterns

### Reaction begins before the partner finishes
A character may understand the point **before the partner finishes** speaking; the eyes, breath, posture, or hands can begin to answer early.

### Thought before word
Before a difficult reply, allow visible cognition: a tiny pause, gaze shift, interrupted action, or breath change before speech.

### Assessment moment
Important news, insult, threat, request, or revelation may require an **assessment moment** before the next tactic.

### Suppressed reaction
The character registers the event but actively contains the visible response.

### Displaced reaction into object handling
Instead of overt facial display, reaction may appear in **object handling**: a grip stops, a cup is set down too carefully, a knot is tried again, an object is avoided.

### Reaction to another person's reaction
A character may react not to the original event, but to seeing how another person receives it.

### Ensemble staggering
In a group, reactions do not have to occur simultaneously. Staggering can preserve hierarchy and readability.

### Silent listener still has a playable task
When a **silent listener** is visible and dramatically active, do not leave them as a neutral face waiting for a cue. Give the listener a **playable task** such as testing whether the speaker is truthful, waiting for an opening, protecting the mood, hiding recognition, or deciding whether to intervene.

The task **must come from** locked scene evidence: current objective, relationship, information state, Beat, or Director Craft inference already supported by that evidence. Do not invent a new motive, secret, Reveal, or story fact just to animate the listener. If no supported task is needed, ordinary listening remains free.

## Timing
Use relative dramatic timing, not fixed seconds at this layer.
